The asymmetry

Writing a report became free.
Reading one did not.

In January 2026 curl ended its bug bounty after an eightfold surge in AI-written reports in a single week. The programme had paid out around $90,000 since 2019. It did not fail — it was buried.

To send a report

Free

No account, no cost, no limit. A model can write a convincing one in seconds.

To check one

2–8 hours

A maintainer reads the code, reproduces it, and writes back. OpenSSF's own estimate.

Daniel Stenberg had already required AI disclosure, and already banned the reporters sending slop. Neither worked. Better detection cannot win, because better slop simply raises the cost of checking — so price the asymmetry instead.

How it works

Three verdicts, and the middle one
is the whole design.

The question is not “were you right?”. Most honest security reports are wrong, and a system that punishes being wrong drives out the people it needs most.

  1. Valid

    Bond returned, plus a tier

    The report was real. You are refunded, and you earn a reputation tier that lives on your own ENS name — so the next project already knows you.

  2. Wrong, in good faith

    Bond returned in full

    You tried, and it did not hold up. That costs you nothing at all. Being wrong is not slop.

  3. Fabricated

    Bond kept

    It cited code that does not exist. The deposit goes to a pool that pays reviewers — never to the person who called it fabricated.

The maintainer posts a bond too.

A deposit only researchers pay is not bail, it is a toll. The project stakes one against its own promise to answer, and if the window closes in silence, anyone at all can claim it — the reporter is paid automatically, without having to ask.

The hard question

So who decides
what counts as slop?

Not us — nobody stakes a deposit on a referee who wrote the rules. And not the maintainer alone, who collects the forfeit. The first read comes from a model running inside a sealed chip, and we show you the request id and the provider address that answered, every single time.

The chip never sentences anyone. It offers a first opinion. A human signs the outcome and can overrule it in one click.

The disclosure clock

Letting a deadline die is silent.
Buying more time is not.

Every report carries a 90-day disclosure deadline that expires by itself — nobody deletes it, the network simply stops answering. Extending one is a signed, public act that is announced the moment it happens.
