In January 2026 curl ended its bug bounty after an eightfold surge in AI-written reports in a single week. Not because the programme failed — it had paid out around $90,000. Because a human still needs two to eight hours to check a report that costs nothing to send.

The asymmetry

Producing a report became free. Checking one did not.

8× surge in reports, one week curl, January 2026
2–8h human time per report OpenSSF triage estimate
0 cost to send one before any of this

Daniel Stenberg had already required AI disclosure. He had already banned the reporters sending slop. Neither worked, so he closed the door — and every honest researcher was shut out with the bots. Detection was never going to win: better slop simply raises the cost of checking. So stop detecting, and price the asymmetry instead.

How bail works here

Three verdicts. The middle one is the whole design.

A reporter posts a refundable bond with a report. What happens next depends on one question — and it is not “were you right?”

  1. Valid

    Bond back, in full — and a tier you keep

    The report was real. You are refunded, and you earn a reputation tier that lives on your ENS name, so the next project already knows you.

  2. Good faith

    Bond back, in full

    Being wrong is not slop. Most honest security reports are wrong. A system that punishes being wrong drives out exactly the people it needs, so being wrong costs you nothing at all.

  3. Fabricated

    Bond slashed — to a pool the maintainer cannot touch

    It cited code that does not exist. The bond goes to a triage pool that pays reviewers. The person who called it slop never receives a rupee of it, so nobody profits from a guilty verdict.

And the door swings both ways

The maintainer posts a bond too.

A deposit only researchers pay is not bail, it is a toll. So the project posts one against its own promise to answer. Miss the window and anyone at all can call it — the reporter is paid automatically, without having to ask.

Both sides are bound, or neither side is honest.

The question that kills the idea

“So who decides what counts as slop?”

Not us — nobody would stake a rupee on a referee who wrote the rules. And not the maintainer alone, who collects the fine. The first read comes from a model running inside a sealed chip, and we show you the request id and the provider address that answered, every time.

The chip never sentences anyone. It gives a first opinion. A human signs the outcome, and can overrule it in one click.
